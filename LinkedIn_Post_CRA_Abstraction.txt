Body text (max 1,200 characters, 8–10 lines):

Here's the problem in one story.

Kibirige rides a boda boda in Kampala. He bought an entry-level Android phone. SafeBoda's app runs on it — because that's who their market is.

But SafeBoda's app depends on open-source libraries, cloud SDKs, and app-store requirements governed outside Uganda. When those upstream layers raise their security baselines to comply with the EU Cyber Resilience Act, the cost of supporting older devices spikes. Eventually, the minimum requirements quietly rise. Kibirige's phone stops working.

No EU regulator wrote a rule for Uganda. But the device dies anyway.

I reviewed four East African ICT statutes — Uganda, Tanzania, Rwanda, Kenya. Every one defines "computer" as a single, undifferentiated object. None recognises the layered reality of hardware → firmware → OS → libraries → application.

Meanwhile, our regulators operate in silos. In Uganda, UNBS handles hardware, UCC handles spectrum, and NITA-U handles software certification — all overseeing the same device with zero structural coordination.

The CRA is not a threat. It's a mirror. It shows us that a law which treats a computer as one thing cannot regulate a market that is actually built in layers.

We don't need to copy the CRA. We need our own answer to one question: who is responsible for which layer — and who is watching what happens when the layers above us move?
